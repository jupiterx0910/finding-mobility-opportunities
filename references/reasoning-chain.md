# The Reasoning Chain — Why Every Step Exists

Opportunity Radar is intentionally sequential. It is not a checklist of interesting factors; it is a chain of claims where each claim must survive before the next deserves more capital, time or attention.

```text
A durable need exists
        ↓
Something changes the economics or access
        ↓
Some careers are closer to the opening than others
        ↓
Early behavior, hiring and supplier activity begin to move
        ↓
Real payers confirm willingness to pay
        ↓
An ecosystem begins to form around the activity
        ↓
The market reveals where bargaining power and profit settle
        ↓
A specific operator either can or cannot access that profit pool
        ↓
A cheap paid experiment tests the thesis
        ↓
Repeat payment proves recurrence
        ↓
Repeatable work becomes an owned asset
        ↓
Evidence determines whether to add, hold or kill
```

A broken link matters more than an impressive story elsewhere in the chain.

---

## 1. Structural Need

**Plain-language question:** What outcome do people or organizations keep wanting regardless of the current technology?

Examples: making money, saving time, reducing risk, gaining status, finding customers, hiring talent, moving goods, staying healthy, complying with rules, reducing uncertainty.

**Why start here?** Technology can create excitement without creating demand. A durable need gives the opportunity an economic floor.

**False positive removed:** technology looking for a problem.

---

## 2. Opening Change / Power Redistribution

**Question:** Why can this opportunity exist now when it was harder, more expensive or impossible before?

Look for:

- cost collapse;
- capability moving from large organizations to small teams;
- new distribution;
- new infrastructure;
- regulation or payment rails becoming available;
- a former bottleneck disappearing;
- a new bottleneck becoming scarce;
- adjacent skills being repriced.

**Why:** Old needs do not automatically create new entrepreneurial windows. The opportunity appears when the economics, access or control structure changes.

**False positive removed:** a real need with no new entry window.

---

## 3. Career-to-Founder Transition Fit

**Question:** Which jobs are already accumulating the ingredients of a business?

Look for roles that repeatedly expose a person to:

- payers and budgets;
- recurring customer pain;
- reorder behavior;
- suppliers and channels;
- implementation failures;
- pricing and objections;
- tacit workflow knowledge;
- reputation or trusted access.

**Why:** The same market is not equally accessible to everyone. A B2B salesperson, field technician and software engineer may see completely different opportunities in the same industry.

**False positive removed:** giving everyone the same startup idea.

---

## 4. Early Signals

**Question:** Before revenue becomes obvious, what observable behavior is already moving?

Possible signals:

- new job titles and role diffusion;
- young-user or campus behavior when relevant;
- new supplier categories;
- merchants allocating shelf space or budget;
- companies building teams around the capability;
- early standards or certification activity;
- repeated creator/developer migration;
- new service firms appearing around the bottleneck.

**Why:** Financial statements are lagging indicators. Opportunity discovery often matters before the category is mature enough to appear clearly in financial data.

**False positive removed:** waiting until the best entry window has already closed.

---

## 5. Real Payment

**Question:** Who pays, from which budget, for what measurable result?

Always separate:

`User / Beneficiary / Buyer / Payer`

Strong evidence includes:

- paid pilots;
- recurring purchases;
- replacement of an existing budget;
- renewals;
- measurable ROI;
- willingness to make a concrete commitment.

**Why:** Attention, downloads, compliments, search volume and funding are cheap compared with payment.

**False positive removed:** popularity without a business.

---

## 6. Industry Formation

**Question:** Is the activity becoming an ecosystem rather than a one-off product?

Look for:

- supplier specialization;
- role diffusion beyond R&D;
- standards and certification;
- financing and insurance;
- service and aftermarket layers;
- distributors and integrators;
- tooling and component modularity.

**Why:** A durable industry creates division of labor and new economic niches.

**False positive removed:** a novelty mistaken for a category.

---

## 7. Economic Quality Gate

**Question:** Does growth survive scrutiny on economics?

Check:

- gross and contribution margin;
- CAC and payback;
- retention and repeat purchase;
- paid-traffic dependence;
- returns and refunds;
- cash conversion;
- platform concentration;
- customer and supplier concentration;
- pricing power;
- regulatory and externality debt.

**Why:** Growth can be purchased, subsidized or rented. Revenue quality matters more than headline volume.

**False positive removed:** GMV or user growth that does not become durable economics.

---

## 8. Power Pool

**Question:** Who can keep the profit after competitors enter?

Possible sources of power:

- customer ownership;
- scarce distribution;
- trusted supply;
- proprietary data;
- standards or certification position;
- integration depth;
- switching costs;
- network effects;
- brand;
- scale economics;
- exclusive or hard-to-replicate know-how.

**Why:** Value creation and value capture are different. Many participants create value but few retain economic rent.

**False positive removed:** entering a large value pool from a structurally weak position.

---

## 9. Operator-Specific Fit

**Question:** Why can this specific operator enter better than an average outsider?

Check:

- customer access;
- supplier access;
- trust;
- tacit knowledge;
- first-sale route;
- capital requirements;
- licensing requirements;
- geography;
- time to first cash;
- affordable loss;
- access to the Power Pool.

**Why:** A 95/100 industry can still be a 30/100 opportunity for a specific person.

**False positive removed:** confusing industry attractiveness with personal accessibility.

---

## 10. Paid Pilot

**Question:** Can the key assumption be tested in 30–60 days with real money?

Prefer:

- one narrow customer type;
- one painful workflow;
- manual delivery with AI/automation where useful;
- explicit baseline;
- measurable result;
- meaningful payment.

**Why:** The founder should buy information before buying headcount, inventory or software development.

**False positive removed:** large irreversible commitment based on a story.

---

## 11. Recurrence

**Question:** Why does the same payer pay again?

Recurrence may come from:

- repeated consumption;
- continuous workflow;
- recurring revenue or cost impact;
- compliance/reporting;
- ongoing risk monitoring;
- replenishment;
- new customer acquisition;
- proprietary data accumulation.

**Why:** One sale can be luck, novelty or founder charisma. Recurrence begins to prove a business model.

**False positive removed:** one-off projects mistaken for durable businesses.

---

## 12. Assetization

**Question:** If the founder stops working for 30 days, what valuable asset remains?

Target ladder:

`expertise → service → SOP → automation/agent → proprietary data → software/product/network/brand/channel/equity`

**Why:** High-income self-employment can be excellent, but it is different from ownership that compounds independently of the founder's hours.

**False positive removed:** a busier job mistaken for owned capital.

---

## 13. Real Option

**Question:** What evidence earns the right to invest more, and what evidence kills the thesis?

Pre-write:

- affordable loss;
- leading indicators;
- confirmation indicators;
- kill criteria;
- next irreversible commitment.

**Why:** Under uncertainty, the goal is not perfect prediction. It is asymmetric decision-making: lose little when wrong and expand exposure when evidence improves.

**False positive removed:** conviction without falsifiability.

---

# The Weakest-Link Rule

Do not let a high total score hide a fatal break.

Examples:

- huge TAM + no payer = broken;
- strong payment + inaccessible Power Pool = structurally weak;
- strong market + unaffordable entry = wrong operator;
- first sale + no recurrence = project, not yet a business;
- recurring revenue + no assetization = potentially valuable self-employment, not yet owned capital.

Always state the weakest link explicitly.
