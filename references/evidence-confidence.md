# Evidence Coverage & Confidence

A score is not a probability.

Opportunity Radar therefore separates four different questions:

```text
Opportunity Score
≠ Evidence Coverage
≠ Evidence Quality
≠ Confidence
```

A thesis can be attractive while still being weakly evidenced. The correct response to that situation is usually a smaller, cheaper test — not fake precision.

---

# 1. Opportunity Score

The existing Signal Confirmation Score and Mobility Opportunity Score answer:

- **How attractive does the opportunity look under the framework?**
- **How good is the operator-specific fit?**

They do **not** mean “probability of success.”

Never write `82/100 = 82% chance of success`.

---

# 2. Evidence Coverage

Evidence Coverage asks:

> **How much of the decision-relevant evidence stack has actually been observed?**

For each applicable scorecard factor, mark evidence status:

- `1.0 = sufficient direct evidence`
- `0.5 = partial / indirect evidence`
- `0.0 = missing`
- `N/A = genuinely not applicable at this stage`

Use factor weights from the relevant scorecard:

`Evidence Coverage = Σ(weight × evidence_status) / Σ(applicable weight) × 100`

Do not mark a factor `N/A` merely because evidence is inconvenient to find.

## Critical factors that usually cannot be ignored

For an actual startup recommendation, these normally require explicit treatment:

- payer / budget;
- operator access;
- legal / ethical portability;
- paid-test feasibility;
- recurrence;
- Power Pool / value capture;
- regulatory or externality risk when material.

An early-stage industry can legitimately lack IPO or public-market evidence. Explain the stage effect rather than pretending the evidence exists.

---

# 3. Evidence Quality

Coverage asks whether evidence exists. Quality asks whether it deserves trust.

Use this practical hierarchy:

| Grade | Evidence type | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | direct / primary / transaction-level | paid invoices, renewals, customer budget, official filings, primary policy, first-party product usage, signed contracts |
| **B** | strong operational proxy | job postings, supplier orders, credible industry data, direct buyer interviews, channel checks |
| **C** | indirect / narrative | media coverage, search trends, social buzz, investor commentary, anecdotal enthusiasm |
| **D** | unsupported | intuition, repeated claims with no traceable evidence, model-generated assertions |

Rules:

- one Grade A payer signal can matter more than ten Grade C attention signals;
- multiple sources that all repeat the same original claim count as one evidence chain, not independent confirmation;
- direct customer payment outranks stated intent;
- current evidence outranks stale evidence when the category moves quickly.

---

# 4. Confidence

Confidence is a judgment about **how much the current conclusion should be trusted**, not a synonym for score.

Default guide:

| Confidence | Typical condition |
|---|---|
| **High** | broad coverage, mostly A/B evidence, payer and recurrence observed, no unresolved fatal link |
| **Medium** | meaningful evidence exists but one or more important layers remain indirect, early or unproven |
| **Low** | sparse coverage, narrative-heavy evidence, critical payer/recurrence/power assumptions untested |

Mechanical thresholds may help as a warning system, but must not override reasoning:

- `80%+ coverage` can support High confidence only if critical factors are directly evidenced;
- `55–79% coverage` is usually Medium at best;
- `<55% coverage` is usually Low, even if the opportunity score is high.

A single critical unknown can downgrade confidence regardless of coverage.

---

# 5. The Four-Number Readout

Prefer this format:

```text
Signal Confirmation Score   76/100
Mobility Opportunity Score  84/100
Evidence Coverage           61%
Confidence                  Medium
```

Then state:

```text
Weakest Link: repeat purchase not yet observed
Key Unknown: whether budget survives after pilot
```

This prevents a precise-looking score from hiding weak evidence.

---

# 6. Confidence-Adjusted Action

Use the combination, not one number:

| Attractive thesis | Evidence coverage | Default action |
|---|---|---|
| High | High | **START / SCALE cautiously** |
| High | Medium | **BUY A REAL OPTION** |
| High | Low | **BUY A SMALL OPTION / research the missing critical evidence** |
| Medium | High | **WATCH or redesign the wedge** |
| Low | Any | **REJECT / redesign** unless a cheap experiment can resolve the specific weakness |

The purpose of uncertainty is not to stop action. It is to control **position size**.

---

# 7. Unknowns Ledger

Every substantive analysis should list 1–5 decision-relevant unknowns.

For each unknown:

| Unknown | Why it matters | Cheapest evidence | Deadline / trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| | | | |

Good unknowns can change the verdict.

Bad unknowns are trivia that would not affect action.

---

# Core Rule

> **Do not make the score more precise than the evidence.**

When evidence is weak, lower confidence, reduce commitment and design the cheapest experiment that can remove the most important uncertainty.
