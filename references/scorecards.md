# Scorecards

Use two separate 100-point scores. Never collapse external evidence and personal fit into one number.

# 1. Signal Confirmation Score — 100

| Factor | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Real payment / retention | 20 |
| Talent flow / role diffusion | 15 |
| Product / supply-chain formation | 10 |
| Capital flow quality | 10 |
| Power redistribution | 10 |
| Formalization / payment rails | 10 |
| IPO / capitalization evidence | 8 |
| Public-market-quality evidence | 7 |
| Geographic / customer diffusion | 5 |
| Persistence | 5 |
| **Total** | **100** |

Interpretation:

- **80–100**: strong confirmation
- **65–79**: emerging but credible
- **50–64**: hypothesis; validate cheaply
- **<50**: narrative-heavy

Early-stage categories may legitimately score low on IPO/public-market evidence. Explain stage effects rather than mechanically penalizing them.

Youth Migration and Campus Frontier signals do **not** receive standalone points. They may support diffusion, persistence and opportunity generation, but never substitute for payment or retention.

# 2. Mobility Opportunity Score — 100

| Factor | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Structural demand | 8 |
| Career-to-founder transition quality | 12 |
| Founder / operator edge | 12 |
| Accessibility / affordable loss | 10 |
| Clear recurring payer | 12 |
| Time to first cash | 8 |
| Nonlinear leverage | 8 |
| Assetization path | 12 |
| Power Pool access | 8 |
| Competition window | 4 |
| Supply elasticity / capital-cycle risk | 3 |
| Regulation / Externality Debt | 3 |
| **Total** | **100** |

Interpretation:

- **85–100**: START a paid pilot
- **75–84**: BUY A REAL OPTION
- **65–74**: WATCH unless fit is exceptional
- **55–64**: may be a career/self-employment opportunity, not yet an ownership opportunity
- **<55**: REJECT or redesign

# Career-to-Founder Transition Subscore

When useful, score each 0–5:

- payer proximity;
- transaction frequency;
- tacit knowledge;
- supplier/channel access;
- ethical/legal portability;
- low-cost testability;
- recurrence;
- assetization potential.

Do not interpret this as a statistical probability of startup success. The purpose is to locate friction and the weakest link.

# Decision Matrix

| Signal Confirmation | Mobility Opportunity | Default |
|---|---|---|
| High | High | START / SCALE |
| High | Low | work there, invest, or sell picks-and-shovels rather than start the core business |
| Low | High | BUY A SMALL REAL OPTION / paid pilot |
| Low | Low | REJECT |

# Hard Vetoes

Reject or redesign when any of these dominates:

1. Startup capital, licensing or R&D exceeds realistic personal balance-sheet capacity.
2. No credible commercial validation path exists within roughly 24–36 months for the operator's goals.
3. Revenue remains linearly dependent on founder labor and no assetization path exists.
4. The Power Pool sits elsewhere with no credible path to access it.
5. Regulatory or Externality Debt can erase expected margin.
6. Supply can expand so quickly that excess returns are unlikely to persist.
7. The thesis depends on taking employer-confidential data, trade secrets, restricted customer lists, code or other non-portable assets.
8. The “customer” likes the idea but no payer, budget or paid commitment can be identified.

# Weakest-Link Override

A weighted score must not hide a fatal break.

Examples:

- market score 90 + no payer → do not START;
- founder fit 90 + legally non-portable edge → redesign;
- strong payment + no recurrence → project, not yet a durable business;
- recurring demand + inaccessible Power Pool → treat as weak ownership opportunity.

Always state the weakest link next to the score.

# Unit Economics

Use:

`Required customers = ceil((T + F) / (P × CM))`

Where:

- `T` = target annual owner income / profit
- `F` = annual fixed operating cost
- `P` = annual revenue per customer
- `CM` = contribution margin

Example:

- target owner income `T = ¥200,000`
- fixed cost `F = ¥60,000`
- annual revenue/customer `P = ¥48,000`
- contribution margin `CM = 80%`

`ceil((200,000 + 60,000) / (48,000 × 0.8)) = 7 customers`

A handful of recurring high-quality B2B customers can be structurally superior to thousands of low-ticket users.
