# Business Design

# Payer Architecture

Never confuse usage with budget.

| Role | Question |
|---|---|
| User | Who uses it? |
| Beneficiary | Who receives the value? |
| Buyer | Who approves the purchase? |
| Payer | Whose budget actually pays? |
| Pain | What recurring problem is being solved? |
| Existing alternative | What do they do today? |
| ROI | Why is the new offer economically better? |

For B2B, prefer recurring problems tied to:

- revenue;
- cost;
- customers;
- talent;
- compliance;
- risk;
- cash flow;
- uptime;
- inventory;
- quality;
- speed.

# First Sale

Before software, brand-building or heavy inventory:

1. List 10 warm or realistically reachable prospects.
2. Select one narrow recurring pain.
3. Identify the existing budget or spend being replaced/improved.
4. Offer a 30–60 day paid pilot or paid order.
5. Define exact deliverables and baseline.
6. Deliver manually with AI/automation where helpful.
7. Measure economic value.
8. Convert to recurring pricing only after value is demonstrated.

Do not build software first unless software itself is the minimum credible test.

# Paid Evidence Hierarchy

From weaker to stronger:

```text
compliment
< stated interest
< meeting / introduction
< data access / workflow access
< signed pilot
< meaningful payment
< repeat payment
< referral / expansion
```

# Recurrence

A customer should pay again because value recurs through one or more mechanisms:

- replenishment;
- revenue generation;
- cost reduction;
- continuous workflow;
- maintenance;
- compliance / reporting;
- risk monitoring;
- proprietary data;
- switching cost;
- channel / distribution access.

If there is no reason for the same payer to pay again, the model is fragile.

# Assetization Ladder

Target:

`personal expertise → service → SOP → automation/agent → proprietary data → software/product/network/brand/channel/equity`

Ask:

> If the founder stops selling personal hours for 30 days, what valuable asset remains?

If the answer is “nothing,” the business is still self-employment. That can still be a valid goal, but it should not be mislabeled as owned capital.

# Career-Founder Transition Rule

When moving from employment to a business:

- use general expertise and legitimate personal reputation;
- do not use employer trade secrets, confidential data, proprietary code, restricted customer lists or other non-portable assets;
- avoid contractual conflicts and verify non-compete/non-solicit/IP obligations where relevant;
- prefer a test that can be run ethically and transparently.

# 6–12 Month Validation Sequence

## Month 0–2

- interview 10–20 qualified payers;
- identify current alternatives and budgets;
- close one paid pilot/order.

## Month 2–4

- deliver;
- measure ROI;
- close customer 2–3;
- record what remains bespoke.

## Month 4–6

- standardize SOP;
- automate repeatable work;
- introduce recurring pricing/reorder mechanisms;
- identify the first proprietary asset.

## Month 6–12

- reach a pre-defined proof threshold such as 5–10 recurring customers or repeat orders;
- reduce founder hours per unit of customer value;
- build proprietary process/data/product/channel advantage;
- choose SCALE / PIVOT / KILL.

# Power-Building Questions

Once payment is proven, ask:

- what can competitors copy tomorrow?
- what becomes stronger with each customer/order?
- what data or process accumulates?
- can the business own customer access rather than rent it?
- can supply reliability become a moat?
- can a service become embedded in a recurring workflow?
- can repeated demand justify a branded/private-label product?

Do not confuse first revenue with durable power.
