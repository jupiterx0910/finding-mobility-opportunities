# Backtest — Daily Deals

> Analytical reconstruction of the Groupon-era daily-deals model. The category had real customers and real transactions; the failure mode was economic defensibility, not absence of demand.

## 1. Contemporaneous Thesis

The thesis was:

`local merchant inventory + email distribution + discounts → repeat consumer demand → scalable local commerce platform`

The early demand signal was real and easy to observe.

## 2. Capital Heat

**Capital Heat:** C3–C4 during the strongest expansion period.

Funding, rapid geographic expansion and public-market attention confirmed that investors saw a scalable category.

But the capital heat also encouraged aggressive acquisition and market expansion before durable merchant economics were proven.

## 3. Payment Evidence

Strong at the transaction level.

Consumers paid and merchants participated. The harder questions were:

- did merchants earn enough incremental profit to repeat?
- did consumers retain the platform without constant discounts?
- who owned the customer relationship?
- how expensive was acquisition?

## 4. Industry Formation

Distribution and merchant acquisition scaled quickly, but differentiation was weak.

Low switching costs and coupon/discount dependence created structural pressure.

## 5. Operator Opportunity

A small operator could have built a focused local service, but the generic “copy Groupon locally” thesis was poor because the business depended heavily on distribution scale and merchant acquisition economics.

Better wedges:

- verticalized local commerce;
- merchant software;
- repeat-customer infrastructure;
- booking / CRM / loyalty;
- specialized demand aggregation.

## 6. Power Pool

The key question was customer ownership.

If the platform rented attention through email or paid acquisition while merchants could leave and consumers could shop elsewhere, the Power Pool was weak.

## 7. Window

**Window:** Opening → Crowding → Consolidating.

## 8. Counter-Signals

- discount dependency;
- merchant dissatisfaction with economics;
- low switching costs;
- customer acquisition pressure;
- platform dependence on continuously acquiring new merchants and customers;
- difficulty converting transaction volume into durable customer ownership.

## 9. Earliest Kill Signal

When merchants cannot show profitable repeat behavior after the initial promotion, treat transaction volume as weak evidence of durable demand.

## 10. What Was Unknowable

The precise lifetime value of merchant relationships and the speed of competitive imitation varied by geography and vertical.

## 11. Radar Verdict

**REJECT generic clone; WATCH / START only where a vertical workflow creates repeat merchant economics and owned customer relationships.**

### Lesson

> Real transactions are necessary but not sufficient. Ask who owns the customer, who keeps the margin, and whether the payer wants to repeat without subsidies.
