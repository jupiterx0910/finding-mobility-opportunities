# Historical Backtest — E-commerce

> Backtest purpose: test the framework using signals that could have been observed before the category became obvious. This is not a claim of deterministic prediction.

## Early structural need

Consumers and merchants already wanted wider product selection, lower search costs, better price discovery and easier distribution.

## Opening change

Internet access, online catalogs, digital payments, logistics networks and marketplaces progressively lowered the cost of matching buyers and sellers.

## Career groups naturally positioned

- merchants and traders;
- catalog/direct-response sellers;
- logistics operators;
- software/web builders;
- category buyers with supplier access.

## Early observable signals

Before mature public-market proof, one could observe:

- rapidly expanding internet usage;
- merchants experimenting with online sales;
- payment/logistics infrastructure improving;
- new marketplace roles and service providers;
- repeat purchases beginning to appear in suitable categories.

## Payment confirmation

The crucial signal was not website traffic. It was real transactions and repeat purchase.

## Industry formation

The ecosystem expanded into:

- marketplaces;
- merchant software;
- digital payments;
- logistics and fulfillment;
- advertising;
- cross-border services;
- analytics;
- warehousing.

## Power Pool

Over time, power concentrated in positions controlling:

- buyer traffic and discovery;
- payment rails;
- logistics density;
- merchant tooling;
- customer data and brand.

A small seller could create value while still having weak bargaining power against a dominant platform.

## Mobility window

The attractive small-operator window was not “build the entire internet.” It was often:

`existing product/supplier edge → online distribution → repeat customers → owned brand/channel/data`

## What the framework should have warned about

- traffic rented from one platform;
- price competition with no differentiation;
- low gross margin after fulfillment/returns;
- inventory growth faster than cash generation;
- customer relationship controlled entirely by the marketplace.

## What could not have been known early

- which platform would dominate each geography;
- exact long-run fee structures;
- which categories would concentrate fastest;
- which individual firms would win.

## Backtest conclusion

Opportunity Radar should not have predicted a specific winner. It should have recognized a **distribution capability downshift** and directed merchants/operators toward small, paid, repeatable experiments while warning that customer ownership and platform dependence would determine long-run economics.
